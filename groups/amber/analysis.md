# Amber: Analytical Approach

## Definition

Attention to energy = substantive discussion of the production, supply, transformation, distribution, consumption, price, conservation/efficiency, security, or transition of energy as a fuel-and-power system. This includes oil and petroleum, gasoline, natural gas, coal, electricity and grids, hydroelectricity, civilian nuclear/atomic power, solar, wind, renewable and alternative fuels, energy efficiency, and energy-related infrastructure. Geopolitical discussion counts when an energy resource itself is substantively at issue (oil supply, embargoes, import dependence, control of oil resources). Excluded: metaphorical uses of *energy* and *fuel* ("energy and drive", "fuel the flames"); conservation of land/forests/wildlife; generic climate/environment discussion without an energy connection; nuclear weapons, deterrence, proliferation, testing, and arms control. Early atomic references count only when they concern civilian/peaceful power development, not atomic diplomacy — so the construct is applied identically to 1950s "Atoms for Peace" talk and 2010s clean-energy talk.

## Measurement

**The submitted estimate is the per-year median of four independently constructed measures** of the definition above — a deliberate ensemble: no single codebook produces the submitted number, and we state that explicitly. The four measures, all built on the unmodified `corpus.csv`:

1. **Contextual clause classifier (word share).** Text split at sentence endings and semicolons (semicolon splitting matters: SOTU addresses contain long multi-topic policy lists). A clause is coded energy on high-precision energy-system expressions with contextual disambiguation (weapons-context nuclear excluded; a conservative second pass recovers pipelines/batteries/hydrogen/drilling only adjacent to an already-coded energy clause). Estimate = energy-clause word tokens / document word tokens. Robustness variants (±20-word local window; weakly supervised TF–IDF + logistic regression at 0.85 threshold) correlated 0.992 and 0.984 with it.
2. **Era-aware dictionary (sentence share).** 20,813 sentences; a keyword lexicon spanning 1946–2020 vocabulary (atomic power → shale) with disambiguation rules: *energy* counts only in energy-NP contexts, nuclear-weapons contexts excluded, metaphorical fuel filtered, embargo/drilling require fuel context.
3. **Exhaustive LLM sentence labels (sentence share).** Eight parallel coding agents labeled every sentence energy/not-energy under a fixed written codebook (the definition above plus explicit trap rules); estimate = labeled share per year.
4. **Embedding concept axis (sentence share).** All sentences embedded (MiniLM, 384-d); a TCAV-style concept activation vector (logistic regression on the LLM labels, held-out accuracy 0.92) projects every sentence on an "energy axis"; estimate = share above the best-F1 threshold τ = 0.20.

The median of the four (for four series, the mean of the middle two) is robust to any single method's idiosyncrasies, stays in [0, 1], and is honestly described as *the consensus of four measures*. It correlates Pearson 0.97–0.99 (Spearman 0.85–0.96) with each input series. Estimates are unsmoothed.

**Unit caveat, stated openly:** measure 1 is a word-token share while measures 2–4 are sentence shares. The two units track each other closely here (pairwise Pearson 0.95–0.98), and the median is dominated by whichever measures agree in a given year, but the submitted series should be read as "share of the address devoted to energy," not as a count of either unit exactly.

## Agent workflow

Two coding-agent tracks ran independently and were merged. Track A (clause classifier): the agent inspected corpus structure and terminology, implemented three estimators, compared them, and audited ambiguous classifications; consequential rules were made explicit rather than letting the agent assign unexplained percentages. Track B: a five-discipline agent panel (political science, computational linguistics, statistics, ML engineering, energy history) converged on the construct and design; agents then built the dictionary, fanned out to label all 20,813 sentences under the fixed codebook, and trained the embedding axis on those labels. Group members intervened to: set the construct boundary (energy-systems framing of climate; civilian vs weapons nuclear), choose sentence/clause units and share denominators, fix segmentation errors surfaced by inspection (a 1991 multi-topic list overcounted by naive sentence splitting), select the ensemble-median as the submitted estimate, and merge the two tracks' documentation. Revisions were driven by observed classification errors, never by forcing the series toward an expected shape.

## Checks

Convergent validity: all pairwise correlations among the four series are Pearson 0.93–0.98; the median correlates 0.97–0.99 with each. Face validity: 1975 is the maximum (0.241); 1980 (0.151) ≫ 1979 (0.006 — Carter's 1979 energy rhetoric was not in the January SOTU); Reagan-era mean (0.004) ≪ Carter-era mean (0.071); a 2009–2014 clean-energy plateau (2012 = 0.091); near-zero 1960s floor. Manual audits: ~80 ambiguous clauses spot-checked in track A; disagreement sentences read in track B. As an external post-estimation sanity check only (never used to fit anything), the series was compared against CPI-deflated real oil prices and oil-price inflation. The embedding analysis also localized energy talk in semantic space (see `figure_maps.png`): a large "energy as economics & technology" cluster and a distinct "energy as security" pocket (atomic diplomacy 1946–55, oil dependence) sitting inside the Cold War / war regions.

## Important choices

(1) Submitting the four-measure median rather than one method — trading a single transparent codebook for robustness, and saying so. (2) Share denominators (words or sentences) rather than raw counts, making written and spoken addresses comparable. (3) Clause/sentence units with semicolon splitting rather than paragraphs. (4) Precision over recall for ambiguous terms; explicit energy connection required for conservation, climate, and ambiguous technologies. (5) Counting civilian nuclear power while excluding weapons/arms control — the rule that most affects 1946–1960. (6) Including energy-motivated security discussion (embargoes, oil dependence).

## Limitations

No hand-coded gold standard exists; validation rests on inter-method agreement, face validity, and limited manual audits. The methods disagree most before 1960, where the embedding axis reads postwar fuels/power-development content higher than rule-based methods — early-period estimates are the least certain. The ensemble median inherits a mixed estimand (word vs sentence shares) and cannot be reproduced from any single documented rule set; users wanting a single-codebook series should use the clause measure or the LLM-label series, both included in `code/`. Rule-based components can miss indirect discussion using no recognizable energy vocabulary; model-based components can encode opaque boundary decisions. The corpus mixes spoken addresses with two much longer written messages (1946, 1973); normalization handles length but not genre.
