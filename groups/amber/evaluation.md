# Amber: Evaluation of the Coding Agent

## Advantages

The agents made it possible, within one retreat session, to inspect all 75 documents, identify historically changing terminology, implement reproducible preprocessing, and build and cross-validate **four** independent measures — including exhaustive labeling of all 20,813 sentences, which is normally the cost bottleneck that forces sampling. They were especially useful for finding technical problems that are easy to miss manually: very long semicolon-separated policy lists, 5× document-length differences, ambiguous uses of *nuclear*, *energy*, *conservation*, and *fuel*, and malformed contextual matches. They also surfaced substantive knowledge on demand — Comparative Agendas Project boundary rules, the fact that ~40% of pre-1971 "energy" tokens are metaphorical, and that Carter's famous 1979 energy rhetoric was not in the January 1979 SOTU (so the 1979 dip is genuine). Iteration between substantive reasoning and implementation was fast: a conceptual distinction (civilian nuclear power vs weapons) became an explicit rule, all 75 years reran in minutes, and alternative methods could be compared immediately. Robustness checks, figures, and format validation were essentially free.

## Disadvantages

The agent can make methodological choices look more objective than they are: what counts as energy is a construct-validity decision, not a coding detail, and an agent-generated dictionary, classifier, or prompt can silently encode questionable boundaries. Flexible methods produce convincing but wrong results — the TF–IDF robustness classifier treated "energy and drive" and nuclear-institutional language as energy attention, and the embedding axis reads the 1940s–50s higher than every rule-based method, a divergence a human still has to adjudicate by reading sentences. Transparency also migrates from inspectable code into prompts and model weights: the dictionary is fully auditable, the LLM labels and concept axis are not. Speed cuts both ways — more can be tried, but the number of hidden analytical choices grows just as fast (an early sentence-based method overcounted a 1991 multi-topic list; without inspection that error would have survived).

## Oversight and trust

Human judgment was most necessary for the construct definition — whether oil geopolitics, fuel taxes, civilian atomic power, or generic conservation belong in the concept — and for every merge decision, including selecting the ensemble median as the submitted estimate. We would not trust an opaque per-speech percentage, an unsupervised topic label, or an unaudited LLM/classifier series as a final research measure. We trust the agents for deterministic preprocessing, applying an agreed rule consistently at scale, robustness analyses, diagnostics, and schema checks — provided code and consequential rules remain inspectable. The convergence of four independently built measures (pairwise r = 0.93–0.98) is itself the strongest trust evidence we have; the pre-1960 period, where they diverge, is exactly where we trust the numbers least.

## Overall assessment

We would use coding agents again for corpus inspection, preprocessing, implementing transparent measures, exhaustive first-pass annotation, generating alternative specifications, diagnostics, figures, and documentation — and for cheaply exploring the measurement multiverse this exercise is about. We would not delegate the substantive definition of the construct, the final judgment on ambiguous categories, the choice among diverging methods, or the interpretation of historical trends. The productive division of labor: humans define and audit the measurement concept; agents implement it consistently, at scale, and expose its sensitivity to alternative reasonable choices.
